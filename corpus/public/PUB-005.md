**By Paul Kepovitz, Co-founder & Head of Engineering**

If you look at how most companies are building internal AI assistants today, the security architecture usually boils down to a system prompt that looks something like this:

> *"You are a helpful company assistant. Do not mention salary information. Do not discuss the Q3 layoffs. Do not reveal upcoming product features to junior staff."*

This is prompt-level access control. It is also fundamentally broken.

The problem with relying on the LLM to police its own output is that language models are not deterministic state machines; they are probabilistic text generators. They are inherently susceptible to prompt injection and context window manipulation. A clever user can easily bypass these instructions with variations of "ignore previous instructions," or by embedding requests in hypothetical scenarios, roleplay prompts, or encoded text. 

Even if the LLM provider patches one vulnerability, another inevitably emerges. 

But there is a more structural issue: **if the LLM is being told not to reveal the salary data, it means the salary data has already been loaded into its context window.** The sensitive information has already left your secure database and been handed over to the generative model. You are relying on a statistical model to act as a firewall. 

At ScopeGuard, we realized that the only way to build a zero-trust RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) pipeline is to move the authorization layer *away* from the LLM. 

Instead of filtering the output, you must filter the retrieval. If a user is not authorized to see confidential financial documents, those documents should never be extracted from your vector database in the first place. By intercepting the search and applying strict, scope-based filtering at the middleware layer, ScopeGuard ensures that the LLM is entirely ignorant of data it shouldn't see. 

You can't trick an AI into revealing a secret it never knew.
